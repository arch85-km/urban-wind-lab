Urban Wind Lab · In brief

Method notes in brief

The short version: what the tool solves, and how far the numbers can be trusted. Treat the output as a qualitative picture of flow behaviour — the velocity ratios are meaningful, the absolute speeds are not validated. The full method notes set out the solver constants, the boundary conditions and the resolution tables in full.

Audience Architecture students and staff Method D3Q19 lattice Boltzmann · BGK · Smagorinsky Runs Entirely in the browser Version 1.0.0 · 15 September 2026

Three things to know before you cite a number

  1. The velocities are qualitative, not predictive. The solve never uses the viscosity of air. Its viscosity is a lattice quantity, ν = 0.00333, chosen for stability, and nothing in the code maps it to a physical fluid — so the district is not being solved at its real Reynolds number. What makes the result useful anyway is that separation from sharp edges is fixed by geometry. That holds for rectilinear massing and fails for curved or streamlined forms.
  2. “Pedestrian level · 1.5 m” is a cell, not a height. The plane is drawn at exactly 1.5 m, but the statistics come from the nearest cell centre — 1.07 m to 1.88 m depending on resolution. The panel displays the height actually sampled. Report that number.
  3. The comfort classes are indicative labels, not a Lawson assessment. The seven bands are the standard LDDC set and the thresholds are right; the method is not. The criteria ask whether a threshold is exceeded more than a stated share of the year, summed over every direction weighted by its annual frequency, using a gust-equivalent speed. This tool applies them to a single instantaneous mean, for one direction, with no gust factor and no exceedance statistic.

What it computes

A genuine three-dimensional lattice-Boltzmann solve — D3Q19 velocity set, BGK collision, Smagorinsky sub-grid turbulence — running in a Web Worker so the interface stays responsive. It is not a 2D slice and not a precomputed lookup: vertical motion, downwash and updraught, is solved rather than inferred. Four quantities are computed per cell — three velocity components and density. Everything else on screen is derived from those: speed, the signed components, pressure coefficient Cp, amplification against the 10 m reference, streamlines and probes.

There is no wall damping on the Smagorinsky term, so eddy viscosity does not vanish correctly at a surface and near-wall shear is over-damped. Ground-level values are the least reliable part of the field.

The domain is fixed, and it is tight

240 × 240 × 90 m, whatever the preset — only the cell count changes, giving cells of 2.14 m to 3.75 m. For the built-in sample district that leaves 26 m of air above the tallest building, and in one direction the geometry reaches the boundary exactly. The silhouette facing the wind fills roughly a quarter to a third of the domain’s cross-section.

The top of the domain is driven, not free: the undisturbed profile is imposed 26 m above the tallest building rather than allowed to respond to it, so air displaced upward has nowhere to go and is pushed through the gaps instead. Terrain roughness shapes the inlet profile only — the ground inside the domain is plain no-slip with no wall function, so selecting “dense city centre” changes what enters the domain, not the drag inside it.

These are measurements, not a verdict. Published practice does set minimum clearances and a maximum blockage, but the tool implements and cites none of it — so neither the full notes nor this summary judge the figures against a standard.

What the cell size costs

The smallest resolvable feature is one cell, so a balcony, a canopy, a parapet or a railing simply does not exist in the solve. A whole building can be only a handful of cells wide: the sample district’s landmark tower spans 8.8 cells at the default preset. All geometry is voxelised, so every surface is staircased and curved facades become steps — which moves where the flow separates.

Not modelled at all

Thermal effects — the solve is isothermal, so no buoyancy, no stack effect, neutral conditions only. Vegetation and porosity; every solid is a perfect no-slip block. Terrain: the ground is a flat plane. More than one wind direction at a time. Turbulence quantities: no vorticity, no turbulence intensity, no pollutant transport. Gusts and transients. Driving rain, snow drifting and acoustics. And validation of any kind.

An EPW import builds a 16-sector wind rose from all 8 760 hourly records, but the solve then uses a single speed and direction: the annual frequencies are displayed, never applied to the results.

Convergence: watch the residual, not the badge

The solve never terminates — there is no stopping criterion and no fixed iteration count. The Steady state badge appears once the residual falls below its threshold, which at the default preset and 8 m/s is about 4 seconds of simulated time: roughly 13% of the time a parcel of air needs to cross the domain. The badge can therefore appear before the flow has traversed the site even once. Let it run.

What you may and may not quote

May
Ratios, and topology
The ratio between two runs at identical settings — which of two massing options produces the windier plaza, and by how much in relative terms. And where the corner jet is, where the wake sits, where flow stagnates, whether a street channels.
May not
A speed, or a comfort class
Not a velocity in m/s at a point as a predicted wind speed — it carries the domain confinement, coarse-lattice error, staircased geometry, an unvalidated Reynolds regime and an instantaneous rather than time-averaged sample. Nor a comfort class as an assessment outcome: it is a threshold applied to a mean, for one direction.

The Lock button on the legend exists for exactly this. Locking the range across two runs is what turns a picture into a comparison — and comparison is where this tool is trustworthy.

Three exercises

Each teaches one principle, states what to build and what to read, and takes ten to twenty minutes. All three are comparative by design, which is where the tool is trustworthy. Lock the legend before you start comparing.

Exercise 01

Downwash, and why podiums exist

  1. Build two OBJ models, identical but for the podium. Scheme A: a single slab block — rectangular, broad face to the wind — 40 × 20 × 60 m (width × depth × height), standing alone. Scheme B: the same slab plus a podium 14 m tall, extending 10 m forward of the windward face, the full 40 m width.
  2. Both at wind 10 m/s normal to the wide face, z₀ = 1.0 m, Balanced, legend Locked to the same range. Import each with OBJ units in metres and fit-to-site off — fitting rescales every model to the same share of the site, flattening the very difference the exercise is built on.
  3. Read the Z plane at 1.5 m, then switch the colour field to Uz and cut a Y plane through the slab centreline.
  4. Pin a probe 5 m in front of the windward face at ground level in each scheme and record the amplification factor. Then vary the podium depth at fixed height, and its height at fixed depth, recording the probe each time.

Principle. In Scheme A the Uz field goes strongly negative down the windward face: high-momentum air from 40–60 m is driven to the ground, producing a fast, uncomfortable strip at the base. That is downwash, the most common wind problem a tall building creates. The podium intercepts the descending flow and moves the acceleration onto the podium roof, where nobody stands. The last step is the real lesson: depth matters more than height, because the podium must separate the descending column from the pavement, not block it.

Exercise 02

Channelling, and why alignment is everything

  1. Use the built-in sample district — no import needed. It has a continuous east–west street.
  2. Run three directions at 8 m/s, z₀ = 1.0 m, Balanced, legend Locked across all three: 270° aligned with the street, 315° oblique, 360° across it.
  3. Turn on the Lawson comfort layer and read the pedestrian-level statistics each time: mean speed, max speed, and the share of area in each band. Note the sampled height the panel reports — it is not 1.5 m.

Principle. The aligned case produces a clear high-velocity ribbon along the canyon: channelling, driven by the pressure gradient along an unobstructed street. The oblique case largely destroys it and replaces it with discrete corner jets at each block end. The cross case leaves the street sheltered but accelerates the gaps between blocks. So channelling is not a property of the street — it is a property of the street and the wind rose together. Which is why a real assessment weights every direction by its annual frequency, and why this tool, running one direction at a time, cannot do that for you.

Exercise 03

Wake, sheltering, and the spacing that controls both

  1. Build three OBJ models, each two identical blocks 40 × 20 × 20 m aligned with the wind, differing only in the gap: 20, 40 and 80 m — that is, spacing-to-height ratios S/H of 1, 2 and 4, where S is the gap and H the 20 m block height.
  2. Wind 8 m/s normal to the blocks, z₀ = 1.0 m, Balanced, legend Locked, tracers on. Import each with OBJ units in metres and fit-to-site off — fitting rescales every model to the same share of the site, flattening the very difference the exercise is built on.
  3. Read a Y plane through the centreline with the speed field, record the calm-area percentage, and describe in one sentence what the air in the courtyard is doing.

Principle. The gap behaves as three different places as it widens, and the transitions are what the exercise is for. At S/H = 1 the flow passes over the top with a single slow rotation trapped between the blocks — skimming flow: calm at ground level, almost nothing exchanging with the air above. At S/H = 2 the shear layer begins to dip in and the calm fraction falls — wake interference. At S/H = 4 the flow has come back down to the ground before it reaches the second block, which stands in something close to open wind — isolated roughness. These are the three classical regimes, and naming them is half the point. Shelter and ventilation pull against each other, and the proportion of the space between buildings is the control you have over both at once. Report what you observed and the S/H at which it changed — not a wind speed.

A caution for all three: compare, do not quote. The velocity ratios between two runs are the finding; the absolute m/s values carry every limitation above. And let each run settle — the Steady state badge appears at about 4 s of simulated time, earlier than the flow has finished developing.

Where it stops

There is no wind-tunnel comparison, no benchmark case and no grid-independence study, in the tool or anywhere behind it. Its output has never been checked against a measured flow. For anything that must inform a real decision about a real site, use a validated solver and a wind engineer. This tool is built to teach why wind behaves as it does around buildings, and it is deliberately small enough to run in a browser — which is the source of every limitation above.

References

The source code cites no literature. It contains no bibliography and no reading list — only names: three methods (D3Q19, BGK, Smagorinsky), one author group in a comment (Hou et al.), one set of comfort criteria (Lawson LDDC) and one file format. The entries below are this page’s identification of where those named things come from, not a record of what was read while the tool was written. Only once does the code name a work at all; everywhere else the step from a method’s name to its paper is an inference made here.

The solver

Bhatnagar, P. L., Gross, E. P. & Krook, M. (1954). ‘A model for collision processes in gases. I’, Physical Review, 94(3), pp. 511–525. doi:10.1103/PhysRev.94.511 — the BGK collision.

Smagorinsky, J. (1963). ‘General circulation experiments with the primitive equations: I’, Monthly Weather Review, 91(3), pp. 99–164. — the sub-grid model, and the constant C = 0.14.

Hou, S., Sterling, J., Chen, S. & Doolen, G. D. (1996). ‘A lattice Boltzmann subgrid model for high Reynolds number flows’, Fields Institute Communications, 6, pp. 151–166. — the τ formula in the collision step, and the one work the code names.

Pedestrian comfort

Lawson, T. V. (1990). The determination of the wind environment of a building complex before construction (Report TVL 9025). University of Bristol. — the comfort categories. Attribution contested: sources differ over whether the LDDC criteria belong to this report or to the City of London. An unpublished report — no DOI, and it could not be checked against a library catalogue.

Further reading — not named by the code
