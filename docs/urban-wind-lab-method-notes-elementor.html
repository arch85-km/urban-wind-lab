Urban Wind Lab Method Notes

What this tool solves, how the solve is scaled to metres and seconds, and — the part that matters most when you cite it — how far the numbers can be trusted. Treat the output as a qualitative picture of flow behaviour: the velocity ratios are meaningful, the absolute speeds are not validated. Everything below is read from the source code or measured by driving the running solver, not from what a CFD package is generally assumed to do. Where the code makes something unclear, this page says so rather than inferring.

Audience Architecture students and staff Method D3Q19 lattice Boltzmann · BGK · Smagorinsky Runs Entirely in the browser Version 1.0.0 · 15 September 2026

Three things to know before you cite a number

  1. The velocities are qualitative, not predictive. The solve never uses the viscosity of air. Its viscosity is a lattice quantity, ν = (τ₀ − ½)/3 = 0.00333, chosen with τ₀ for stability, and nothing in the code maps it to a physical fluid. The district is therefore not being solved at its real Reynolds number. What makes the result useful anyway is that separation from sharp edges is fixed by geometry — the code's own stated assumption. Quote ratios and differences between runs; do not quote a speed in m/s as a predicted wind speed.
  2. The buildings sit close to the domain walls. For the built-in sample district the silhouette facing the wind fills about 35% of the domain's cross-section at the default direction, there is 26 m of air above the tallest building, and in one direction the geometry reaches the boundary exactly. Confinement on that scale pushes flow over roofs and through gaps that would otherwise spill sideways. This page reports those measurements; it does not judge them against any published guideline (see Limitations of this page).
  3. "Pedestrian level · 1.5 m" is a cell, not a height. The plane is drawn at exactly 1.5 m, but the statistics come from the nearest cell centre — 1.07 m to 1.88 m depending on resolution. And the comfort classes are Lawson thresholds applied to a mean speed for one direction: no gust factor, no exceedance statistics, no directional weighting. They are indicative labels, not a Lawson assessment.
01

What it computes

A genuine three-dimensional lattice-Boltzmann solve, running in a Web Worker so the interface stays responsive. It is not a 2D slice and not a precomputed lookup: vertical motion — downwash, updraught — is solved rather than inferred.

PropertyValue in the code
LatticeD3Q19 — 19 velocities, three dimensions
CollisionBGK, single relaxation time
TurbulenceSmagorinsky sub-grid, C = 0.14
Base relaxation timeτ₀ = 0.51
Reference lattice velocityu_ref = 0.045 at 10 m
PrecisionFloat32 distributions · Float64 scratch per cell
 Ten of the nineteen lattice directions carry a non-zero vertical component, which is what makes the vertical field real rather than interpolated.

Viscosity and the sub-grid model

The standard LBM relation gives ν = (τ − ½)/3 in lattice units — 0.00333 at τ₀. Smagorinsky then raises τ locally from the second moment of the non-equilibrium distribution, the Hou et al. formulation, so eddy viscosity appears where the flow is sheared:

τ = ½( τ₀ + √( τ₀² + 18√2 · C² · |Π| / ρ ) ) C = 0.14 τ is clamped to 1.6 stability guard

There is no wall damping on the Smagorinsky term. Eddy viscosity does not vanish correctly at a surface, so near-wall shear is over-damped — one reason ground-level values are the least reliable part of the field.

The viscosity is a lattice parameter, not air

This is the caveat that governs every number on the page, so it is worth being exact about what the code does and does not contain. The source never states a physical viscosity. There is no kinematic viscosity of air in the file, no temperature, and no conversion between the two. What there is, is τ₀ = 0.51, chosen so the scheme stays stable, and the standard LBM relation that turns it into a lattice viscosity.

The consequence is best seen by working the lattice-side Reynolds number for the sample district's landmark tower — 64 m tall on a 24 m square plan — at the default 8 m/s on the Balanced lattice. Every figure here comes from the code:

Quantity, in lattice unitsValueWhere it comes from
Velocity at the tower's mid-height0.066u_ref × the log profile at 32 m
Tower plan width8.8 cells24 m ÷ Δx 2.73 m
Viscosity0.00333(τ₀ − ½)/3
Lattice Reynolds number≈ 1.7 × 10²u × L / ν
 Two hundred, on a lattice. Whatever the equivalent figure is for wind around a real 64 m tower, it is not this, and the Smagorinsky term lowers the effective value further still. The solve is not resolving the physical Reynolds number and does not claim to: it relies on separation being pinned by sharp edges, which the code states as its assumption. That holds for rectilinear massing and fails for curved or streamlined forms.

Convergence — and what "steady state" actually means

A residual is computed at every reporting interval — the mean absolute change in cell speed between reports, normalised by mean speed — and shown on screen. The Steady state badge appears when step > 260 and residual < 0.0022. A divergence guard halts and reports if density leaves 0.2 < ρ < 5.

Note what this is not. The solve never terminates: there is no stopping criterion and no fixed iteration count, and nothing prevents a student reading results at step 20. Worse, the badge is generous. At Balanced and 8 m/s one step is 0.0153 s, so step 260 is ≈ 4.0 s of simulated time — about 13% of the 30 s a parcel of air needs to cross the 240 m domain at the reference speed. The badge can therefore appear before the flow has traversed the site even once. Let it run, and watch the residual rather than the badge.

Units, scaling and the domain

The domain is fixed at 240 × 240 × 90 m. Only the cell count changes between presets; the physical box never does.

 Lattice resolution is the only thing about the domain a student can change. Domain size, τ₀, the Smagorinsky constant, u_ref, the reporting interval and the convergence threshold are all fixed and not exposed.

Mapping to metres and seconds

Δx = 240 / n metres per cell u = 0.045 at z = 10 m lattice units, whatever the physical speed Δt = Δx · u_ref / U₁₀ seconds per step Ma = u_ref · √3 = 0.078 weak-compressibility error O(Ma²) ≈ 0.6%

Changing the wind speed does not re-solve

By design. The stored field is normalised — velocity as U/U₁₀, pressure as Cp — and the reference speed is applied on display. This is the Reynolds-independence assumption again: the flow pattern is taken as fixed and only the magnitudes scale. Changing direction or terrain roughness does start a new solve.

Boundary conditions

BoundaryCondition in the code
Upwind facesEquilibrium at a logarithmic ABL profile
Downwind facesZero gradient — a copy of the interior neighbour
Domain topDriven free stream — equilibrium at the same log profile
GroundHalf-way bounce-back, no-slip
BuildingsHalf-way bounce-back on voxelised solids
U(z) = U₁₀ · ln((z + z₀)/z₀) / ln((10 + z₀)/z₀) z₀ = 0.03 / 0.3 / 1.0 / 2.0 m

Logarithmic, not uniform and not a power law; the +z₀ offset avoids the singularity at the wall. The inlet is applied per cell to whichever faces face upwind (n·w > 0), so any azimuth works without rotating the model — but it also means the lateral faces are not symmetry planes. Each face is either an inlet or a zero-gradient outlet.

Two things worth stating plainly. The top of the domain is driven, not free: the code writes onto it the same equilibrium log profile it writes onto the inlet faces — its own label is “driven free stream” — so the undisturbed wind is being imposed 26 m above the tallest sample building rather than allowed to respond to it. Air displaced upward by the massing has nowhere to go, and is pushed through the gaps instead. And z₀ shapes the inlet profile only — the ground inside the domain is plain no-slip bounce-back with no wall function and no roughness. Selecting "dense city centre" changes what enters the domain, not the drag inside it.

The built-in sample district measures 184.7 × 207.4 m in plan and 64 m to the top of its landmark tower. Set against the fixed 240 × 240 × 90 m box, that leaves:

Published CFD practice does set minimum clearances and a maximum blockage, and these numbers are a long way from them — but the tool neither implements nor cites any such guidance, so this page does not apply one either. What is and isn't modelled

Four quantities are solved per cell — three velocity components and density. Everything else on screen is derived from those.

Lawson comfort class
The seven coded bands are the standard LDDC set: frequent sitting < 2.5, occasional sitting < 4, standing < 6, strolling < 8, business walking < 10, uncomfortable < 15, unsafe ≥ 15 m/s — the last matching the LDDC safety limit for a frail person or cyclist. The thresholds are right; the method is not. The criteria ask whether a threshold is exceeded more than a stated share of the year — 5% for the comfort categories, 0.025% for the safety limit — summed over every wind direction weighted by its annual frequency, using a gust-equivalent rather than a mean speed. This tool applies the numbers to a single instantaneous mean for one direction, with no gust factor and no exceedance statistic at all. The labels are indicative; they are not a Lawson assessment. "Calm area" is separate again: the fraction below an absolute 1.5 m/s.
Partly
The Cp reference
Referenced to the lattice datum ρ = 1, the density imposed at the inlet — so approximately to the undisturbed inflow, rather than to a measured free-stream static pressure at a stated point.
Note
What amplification is against
The 10 m reference speed, not the undisturbed speed at the same height. A value of 0.8 at pedestrian level therefore does not mean "sheltered": for z₀ = 1 m the undisturbed profile at 1.4 m is already only about 0.36 × U₁₀.

Not modelled

These are the assumptions a student is most likely to make wrongly, because a general-purpose CFD package would offer them.

Out
Thermal effects, entirely
The solve is isothermal. No buoyancy, no stack effect, no solar gain, no stratification, no stable or unstable atmospheric conditions. Neutral only.
Out
Vegetation and porosity
No trees, no hedges, no porous screens, no permeable facades. Every solid is a perfect no-slip block.
Out
Terrain
The ground is a flat plane at z = 0. No slopes, no embankments, no surrounding topography.
Out
Multiple wind directions
One direction at a time. There is no multi-direction run and no wind-rose weighting of results.
Out
Turbulence quantities
No vorticity output — the term does not occur in the file — no turbulence intensity, no TKE, no age of air, no pollutant transport.
Out
Gusts and transients
The tool reports an instantaneous field, not a time-averaged one with peaks. No gust factors, no exceedance probabilities, no unsteady statistics.
Out
Moisture, noise, snow
No rain or driving-rain assessment, no snow drifting, no acoustics, no roof-level wind for equipment siting.
Out
Validation
None of any kind. No wind-tunnel comparison, no benchmark case, no grid-independence study.
Note
Climate statistics
An EPW import builds a 16-sector wind rose from all 8 760 hourly records, and clicking a sector loads that sector's mean, 90th-percentile or peak speed into the solver. But the solve then uses that single speed and direction: the annual frequencies are displayed, never applied to the results.
04

How exact the numbers are

The short answer, and the one to give students: qualitative. The tool reliably shows where flow accelerates, separates, recirculates and stagnates, and how those patterns change when the massing changes. That is what it is for. A specific velocity at a specific point should not be quoted as a predicted wind speed.

What the cell size costs you

Cells are 2.14 to 3.75 m across. The consequences are concrete:

  • The smallest resolvable feature is one cell. A 1 m balcony, a canopy, a parapet, a railing or a colonnade simply does not exist in the solve.
  • A whole building can be only a handful of cells wide. At Balanced the landmark tower's 24 m plan is 8.8 cells across, and at Fast it is 6.4. Anything narrower than that is coarser still, and the flow around it is shaped as much by the lattice as by the building.
  • All geometry is voxelised by a surface pass plus a parity fill, so every surface is staircased. Diagonal and curved facades become steps, which moves where the flow separates.
  • Bounce-back places the wall halfway between cells and there is no wall function, so near-wall velocity gradients are resolved by whatever the lattice provides — at these cell sizes, very little.

The approximations, and what each one costs

ApproximationCost
Small fixed domainBoundary interference near buildings; over-acceleration over roofs and through gaps
Coarse latticeSub-cell detail lost; separation points approximate

 Each of these is a deliberate trade for a solve that runs interactively in a browser. Together they are why the absolute numbers are indicative and the comparisons are not.

The pedestrian height is not 1.5 m

The comfort plane is drawn at exactly z = 1.5 m, but the statistics are sampled from the nearest cell centre, round(1.5/Δx − 0.5), which on every preset resolves to layer 0:

PresetHeight actually sampled
Fast1.88 m
Balanced1.36 m
Detailed1.07 m
 So a figure labelled "pedestrian level · 1.5 m" is taken somewhere between 1.07 m and 1.88 m, and it moves when a student changes preset. The panel does display the true sampled height — report that number, not 1.5 m.

One thing that is done carefully: the mean, max, amplification, calm fraction and Lawson distribution are computed over the area of interest — the model bounding box widened by 30 m in plan and 18 m in height, then clamped to the domain — not the whole domain, so the empty buffer around the site does not dilute the statistics.

What a student may and may not quote

  • May: the ratio between two runs at identical settings. Which of two massing options produces the windier plaza, and by how much in relative terms.
  • May: the topology — where the corner jet is, where the wake sits, where flow stagnates, whether a street channels.
  • May not: a velocity in m/s at a point, as a predicted wind speed. It carries the domain-confinement bias, coarse-lattice error, staircased geometry, an unvalidated Reynolds regime and an instantaneous rather than time-averaged sample.
  • May not: a comfort class as an assessment outcome. It is a threshold applied to a mean, for one direction.

The Lock button on the legend exists for exactly this. Locking the range across two runs is what turns a picture into a comparison, and comparison is where this tool is trustworthy.

Bringing in your own data

OBJ import takes units in m, mm, cm, ft or in, with a Y-up toggle and a fit-to-site toggle; the mesh is voxelised on the lattice, as described under How exact the numbers are. EPW import reads all 8 760 hourly records and builds a 16-sector rose, and a sector can be applied at its mean, 90th-percentile or peak speed.

What is fixed, and not exposed

Domain size, τ₀, the Smagorinsky constant, u_ref, the reporting interval and the convergence threshold. Changing any of them means changing the source. Everything in the display group above — colour field, planes, tracers, legend, layers — is presentation: none of it touches the solve.

06

Three exercises

Each teaches one principle, states exactly what to build and what to read, and takes ten to twenty minutes. All three are comparative by design, which is where the tool is trustworthy. Lock the legend before you start comparing.

Exercise 01

Downwash, and why podiums exist

  1. Build two OBJ models, identical but for the podium. Scheme A: a single slab, 40 × 20 × 60 m, standing alone. Scheme B: the same slab plus a podium 14 m tall — four storeys — extending 10 m forward of the windward face, the full 40 m width.
  2. Settings for both: wind 10 m/s, direction normal to the wide face, z₀ = 1.0 m, Balanced, legend Locked to the same range.
  3. Read the Z section plane at 1.5 m, then switch the colour field to Uz and cut a Y plane through the slab centreline.
  4. Pin a probe 5 m in front of the windward face at ground level in each scheme. Record the amplification factor.
  5. Vary the podium depth at fixed height, then its height at fixed depth, and record the probe each time.

Principle. In Scheme A the Uz field goes strongly negative down the windward face: high-momentum air from 40–60 m is driven to the ground, producing a fast, uncomfortable strip at the base. That is downwash, and it is the single most common wind problem a tall building creates. The podium in Scheme B intercepts the descending flow and moves the acceleration onto the podium roof, where nobody stands. The last step is the real lesson: depth matters more than height, because what the podium must do is separate the descending column from the pavement, not block it.

Exercise 02

Channelling, and why alignment is everything

  1. Use the built-in sample district — no import needed. It has a continuous east–west street.
  2. Run three directions at 8 m/s, z₀ = 1.0 m, Balanced, legend Locked across all three: 270° aligned with the street, 315° oblique, 360° across it.
  3. Turn on the Lawson comfort layer and read the pedestrian-level statistics panel each time.
  4. Record mean speed, max speed and the percentage of area in each comfort band. Note the sampled height the panel reports — it is not 1.5 m.

Principle. The aligned case produces a clear high-velocity ribbon along the canyon: channelling, driven by the pressure gradient along an unobstructed street. The oblique case largely destroys it and replaces it with discrete corner jets at each block end. The cross case leaves the street sheltered but accelerates the gaps between blocks. So channelling is not a property of the street — it is a property of the street and the wind rose together. Which is exactly why a real assessment weights every direction by its annual frequency, and why this tool, which runs one direction at a time, cannot do that for you.

Exercise 03

Wake, sheltering, and the spacing that controls both

  1. Build three OBJ models, each two identical blocks 40 × 20 × 20 m aligned with the wind, differing only in the gap: 20 m (S/H = 1), 40 m (S/H = 2), 80 m (S/H = 4).
  2. Settings: wind 8 m/s normal to the blocks, z₀ = 1.0 m, Balanced, legend Locked, tracers on.
  3. Read a Y section plane through the centreline with the speed field, and record the calm-area percentage from the statistics panel.
  4. Watch the tracers in the gap in each case, and describe in one sentence what the air in the courtyard is doing.

Principle. The gap behaves as three different places as it widens, and the transitions are what the exercise is for. At S/H = 1 the tracers show the flow passing over the top with a single slow rotation trapped between the blocks: calm at ground level, and almost nothing exchanging with the air above. At S/H = 2 the shear layer begins to dip in and the calm fraction falls. At S/H = 4 the flow has come back down to the ground before it reaches the second block, which stands in something close to open wind. Have students find where each change happens and connect it to a design decision: shelter and ventilation pull against each other, and the proportion of the space between buildings is the control they have over both at once. Report what you observed and the S/H at which it changed — not a wind speed.

A caution for all three: compare, do not quote. The velocity ratios between two runs are the finding; the absolute m/s values carry every limitation in How exact the numbers are. And let each run settle — the Steady state badge appears at about 4 s of simulated time, which is earlier than the flow has finished developing.

07

Limitations of this page

The sections above describe the tool. This one describes the document, because a page students cite should say how it was made and where it stops.

Where each number came from

Every figure on this page falls into one of three classes, and none into a fourth:

Read
Read directly from the source
Constants and thresholds quoted as they appear in urban-wind-lab.html: τ₀ = 0.51, u_ref = 0.045, C = 0.14, the τ clamp at 1.6, the density guard 0.2 < ρ < 5, the badge test step > 260 && residual < 0.0022, the three lattice presets, the seven Lawson bands, the roughness options, the control ranges and defaults, and the statistics margins of 30 m in plan and 18 m in height.
Derived

Δx and Δt per preset; the 4.0 s of simulated time at step 260; the sampled pedestrian heights 1.88 / 1.36 / 1.07 m; the lattice viscosity and lattice Reynolds number; the sample district's bounding box and clearances, obtained by running the tool's own buildSampleCity generator; and the blockage percentages, obtained by projecting its 29 boxes onto the plane normal to each wind direction and rasterising the silhouette at 0.1 m. Arithmetic, not measurement — but arithmetic on the code's own values.
Observed

Behaviour that only appears at run time: that the solve does not terminate, that the badge can be reached before the flow has crossed the site, and that the reported sampled height changes with the resolution preset.
Absent

Nothing. Where a figure would have required an assumption the code does not supply, it was removed rather than estimated — see the next two entries for what that cost. The further-reading list at the foot of the page is deliberately inert in the same spirit: it is cited nowhere and supports nothing, so no figure above depends on a work the tool does not name.

What this page deliberately does not do

It applies no external benchmark. Published guidance exists on how large a CFD domain should be relative to the buildings in it, how many cells a building should span, how a pedestrian comfort criterion should be evaluated across a wind rose, and what a planning authority will accept. The tool implements none of it and cites none of it, so this page cites none of it either. Everything said here about domain size, resolution and convergence is therefore descriptive: these are the measurements, and they are not compared with a standard.

This is a real limitation and worth being blunt about. This page can tell a student what the tool does; it cannot tell them whether that is good enough for any given purpose. Answering that means bringing your own guidance and applying it to the measurements in Units, scaling and the domain and in How exact the numbers are — which is, in fairness, a defensible exercise to set.

Two figures were dropped for the same reason rather than being carried with an assumption: the physical Reynolds number, which would need the kinematic viscosity of air, a value the code never contains; and the vortex-shedding period behind the convergence discussion, which would need a Strouhal number the code never mentions. Both would have strengthened the argument. Neither is in the source, so neither is here.

Two gaps in the provenance list

The references are in two blocks, and the distinction matters. The first records where the methods the code names come from — it is not a record of what was consulted while the tool was written, because the code does not say. The second is further reading, added because provenance is a poor starting point for a student; nothing in it is named by the code, and no claim on this page rests on it. The provenance rule leaves two visible holes, and it is better to name them than to let a reader assume an oversight:

  • The lattice has no citation. The code uses the designation D3Q19 throughout but attributes it to no one, so no entry appears for it. The velocity set and its weights have a standard origin in the literature; this page does not assert which, because the code does not.
  • The comfort thresholds have a contested citation. The code says “Lawson LDDC” and names no document. The bands themselves are not in doubt — they are the standard LDDC set, and they match it band for band, as the table below shows. What is unsettled is whom to credit: some sources give Lawson's 1990 University of Bristol report TVL 9025, others attribute the criteria to the City of London, which adopted them for the Square Mile. The reference list uses the Lawson report and says so on the entry. Treat the attribution, not the numbers, as the open question.

How the references were checked

Each entry was looked up rather than recalled, and the details below agree across several independent indexes. One limit should be recorded: the network available while this page was written could not reach the publishers directly — the DOI resolver, ScienceDirect, arXiv, the AMS journals site and the EnergyPlus documentation host were all blocked — so the entries could not be confirmed against the authoritative record itself. Where a DOI is given it appeared verbatim in an index; no DOI has been constructed from a pattern, and the Lawson entry carries none for exactly that reason. Treat page ranges and volume numbers as good but not publisher-confirmed, and check them if you are citing formally.

And the tool itself is unvalidated

Stated once more here because it belongs with the limitations rather than only in How exact the numbers are: there is no wind-tunnel comparison, no benchmark case, and no grid-independence study, in the tool or anywhere behind it. Its output has never been checked against a measured flow.

Everything on this page was read from the tool's source code or computed from it; Limitations of this page says which figures are which, and by what method. Where a figure would have needed a value the code does not contain, it was removed rather than estimated. Where the code did not settle a question, the page says so rather than inferring.

For anything that must inform a real decision about a real site, use a validated solver and a wind engineer. This tool is built to teach why wind behaves as it does around buildings, and it is deliberately small enough to run in a browser — which is the source of every limitation listed above.

Neither inference is in serious doubt, but both are inferences. Against each entry is the exact term the code uses and where it uses it, so the mapping can be checked rather than trusted. Further reading — not named by the code

Nothing in this second list is named in the source, used by the tool, or relied on anywhere on this page. No claim above rests on any of it. It is here for one reason: the list above is a record of provenance, and provenance makes a poor starting point for a student. Two paywalled mid-century papers, an unpublished university report and a documentation mirror will not teach anyone the method. These will.

Krüger, T., Kusumaatmaja, H., Kuzmin, A., Shardt, O., Silva, G., & Viggen, E. M. (2017). The Lattice Boltzmann Method: Principles and Practice (Graduate Texts in Physics). Springer. ISBN 978-3-319-44647-9; https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-319-44649-3
The current standard text. Covers the D3Q19 velocity set, the BGK collision and the Smagorinsky closure in one place — everything described in What it computes, derived properly rather than summarised.

Lawson, T. V. (2001). Building Aerodynamics. Imperial College Press, 286 pp. ISBN 1-86094-187-7.
Background, not the source of the tool's comfort bands. Lawson's published book, and obtainable, which the 1990 LDDC report is not — but the criteria set out here is a later and different one. The tool implements the LDDC bands, attributed above. Do not cite this book for them.

Blocken, B., Stathopoulos, T., & van Beeck, J. P. A. J. (2016). Pedestrian-level wind conditions around buildings: review of wind-tunnel and CFD techniques and their accuracy for wind comfort assessment. Building and Environment, 100, 50–81. Open copy at pure.tue.nl
